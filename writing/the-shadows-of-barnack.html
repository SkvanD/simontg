The Shadows of Barnack

A Simon & Debbie mystery • 15 chapters • Complete story

Chapter 1: The Weight of the Stone

The limestone of Barnack didn't just sit in the ground; it seemed to breathe. As Simon and Debbie drove the narrow, winding lanes toward the village, the very air felt different—heavier, as if the centuries of history were pressing down on the car's roof.

"It's different from York," Debbie remarked, her eyes tracing the jagged edges of the stone walls that lined the road. "York felt like a grand, ancient city. This… this feels like the earth itself is made of ghosts."

Simon smiled, though his eyes were fixed on the way the shadows stretched across the uneven terrain. "It's the stone, Deb. This stuff built the cathedrals. It's been pulled from these hills for a thousand years. Every building you see here is a piece of the ground moved up to meet the sky."

They were passing through a landscape of sudden rises and deep, unexpected hollows. The Barnack Hills and Holes loomed to their left, a rugged, terraced landscape of former quarries that looked like a giant's playground. The light of the late afternoon was turning the limestone a pale, ghostly gold, but in the deep crevices of the earth, the shadows were already turning black.

Chapter 2: Echoes in the Hollows

The next morning, the air was crisp, carrying the scent of damp earth and ancient dust. They had decided to walk the path through the Hills and Holes, a trek Simon had promised would be "the most beautiful walk in the county."

As they descended into the hollows, the world above seemed to vanish. The steep, terraced sides of the quarries rose up around them, creating a natural amphitheatre of stone. The silence here was absolute, broken only by the occasional call of a skylark or the crunch of their boots on the scree.

"It's incredible," Debbie whispered. She reached out to touch a weathered face of stone. It was rough, pitted by time and the elements, yet it felt strangely warm. "You can almost see the marks of the tools that carved this out. All those men, all those years, just… taking pieces of the world to build something elsewhere."

"It's a landscape of absence," Simon said, looking up at the rim of the quarry. "A place defined by what has been taken away."

He didn't notice the way the light shifted, or the way a single, dark shape seemed to flicker momentarily in one of the deeper, unmapped crevices of the hills.

Chapter 3: The Shadow on the Stone

The peace of the morning was shattered not by a sound, but by the absence of it.

They had been walking near the edge of the old quarry, where the ground sloped steeply toward a narrow, shaded gully. Simon had stopped to adjust his boot when he saw it—a flash of something that didn't belong to the earth. Not the pale cream of the limestone, nor the green of the moss. It was a jarring, synthetic blue.

"Simon, look," Debbie said, her voice trembling.

They approached the edge. Below them, nestled in a shallow depression in the stone, lay a figure. At first, it looked like a discarded piece of fabric, but as they moved closer, the reality of it settled into their chests like lead.

It was a man. He was sprawled unnaturally against the limestone, his face turned toward the sky. But it wasn't the stillness that was terrifying; it was the way the stone seemed to have swallowed him. He looked less like a person and more like a part of the landscape that had been broken off.

"We need to call someone," Debbie said, her hand flying to her mouth.

Simon was already reaching for his phone, but his eyes were fixed on the man's hand. Clutched tightly in his fingers was a small, jagged piece of dark wood—something that looked out of place in the heart of the stone quarries.

The silence of the Hills and Holes was gone, replaced by the frantic thudding of two hearts and the sudden, chilling realisation that the shadows of Barnack were no longer just historical. They were very, very real.

Chapter 4: The Scars of the Earth

The forensics team arrived as the sun began its descent, casting long, skeletal shadows from the limestone cliffs across the body. Simon and Debbie stood behind the yellow tape, watching as the experts moved with practiced, clinical efficiency.

The piece of dark wood—the one Simon had seen clutched in the dead man's hand—had been carefully bagged. It was a fragment of aged, charred oak, looking as if it had been salvaged from a fire that had occurred a century ago.

"It's not just old," the lead investigator, a man named Sergeant Miller, said as he approached them. He looked weary, his eyes scanning the rugged landscape of the Hills and Holes. "It's medieval. The grain, the way it's been treated… this is architectural salvage. Specifically, wood that would have been used in high-status constructions. Churches, manors, perhaps even the old parts of the Minster."

Debbie felt a chill that had nothing to do with the evening air. "You think it's connected to the crime?"

"It's more than that," Miller replied, his voice dropping. "We've had reports of similar fragments being found near old sites around Barnack. It's almost as if someone is leaving a trail of the past behind."

Chapter 7: The Timber's Secret

The investigation deepened as the charred oak was analyzed. Sergeant Miller returned to the Goodwins, his expression more grave than before.

"The wood is indeed from a period of intense construction," Miller explained, showing them a high-resolution photo of the fragment. "But there's something else. The carbon dating is inconsistent with the local sites we've checked. It's as if this piece was intentionally kept, preserved, and then… discarded at a crime scene."

Simon felt a knot tighten in his stomach. "Why would a killer leave a piece of history at a murder site?"

"That's the question," Miller said. "Unless the history itself is the motive."

Chapter 8: The Old Man's Warning

The next day, seeking answers, they visited the village church, St. Peter's. The air inside was heavy with the scent of beeswax and centuries of prayer.

As they walked through the nave, they encountered an old man sitting alone in a pew. His skin was like ancient parchment, and his eyes were milky with age.

"The stone remembers," he whispered as they passed, his voice a dry rasp. "The stone never forgets the blood it has tasted."

"Excuse me, sir?" Simon asked, stepping toward him. "Did you see anything unusual near the quarries last night?"

The old man looked up, and for a moment, his gaze was startlingly clear. "You look for men," he said with a thin, unsettling smile. "But you should be looking for the shadows they cast. The shadows belong to the stone. And the stone is hungry."

Chapter 9: The Shadow of Kingsley House

The unsettling feeling followed them out of the church. As they drove back through the village, they passed Kingsley House, the grand, limestone manor that sat on the edge of the woods.

As the car passed, Debbie glanced up at the second-story window. For a split second, she saw it—a dark, indistinct shape standing perfectly still, watching them pass.

"Simon, did you see that?" she gasped.

Simon looked, but the window was just a dark void, reflecting the grey sky. "It's just the light, Deb. Just the way the shadows fall on old buildings."

But as they drove away, Simon knew he was lying. The house didn't just feel old; it felt expectant.

Chapter 11: The Whispers of St. Peter's

The following morning, the tension in the village was palpable. The news of the discovery in the Hills and Holes had spread through Barnack like a low-frequency hum.

Simon and Debbie decided they needed to see the village church, St. Peter's. If the wood was connected to ancient structures, the church was the logical place to start.

The church sat on a slight rise, its stone walls appearing to grow directly out of the earth. Inside, the air was cool and smelled of beeswax and ancient, undisturbed dust. The light filtered through high, narrow windows, creating pools of soft illumination in the gloom.

As they walked through the nave, Debbie noticed a man sitting in a pew near the altar. He was old, his skin like parchment, his eyes milky with age. He didn't look up as they passed, but he seemed to be muttering under his breath.

"The stone remembers," he whispered, his voice a dry rasp. "The stone never forgets the blood it has tasted."

Simon paused. "Excuse me, sir? Did you see anything near the quarries last night?"

The old man looked up then, and for a moment, his gaze was startlingly clear. "You look for men," he said, a thin, unsettling smile touching his lips. "But you should be looking for the shadows they cast. The shadows belong to the stone. And the stone is hungry."

He went back to his muttering, leaving Simon and Debbie standing in a silence that felt suddenly very heavy.

Chapter 12: The Shadow of Kingsley House

They left the church feeling unsettled, the old man's words echoing in their minds. As they drove back toward the centre of the village, they passed Kingsley House.

It was a grand, imposing structure of weathered limestone, its windows dark and uninviting. The local legends said it was haunted, that the spirits of those who had lived there centuries ago still wandered the halls.

As they passed, Debbie glanced up at the second-storey window. For a split second, she thought she saw a figure—a dark, indistinct shape—standing perfectly still, watching them pass.

"Did you see that?" she whispered, her voice trembling.

Simon looked, but there was nothing there but the reflection of the grey sky in the glass. "It's just the light, Deb. Just the way the shadows fall on old buildings."

But as they drove away, Simon couldn't shake the feeling that the house wasn't just a building. It was a sentinel, a silent watcher waiting for the next piece of the past to be unearthed.

• • •

Chapter 13: The Heart of the House

The suspicion that had been a low hum in the back of Simon's mind turned into a deafening roar. The charred oak, the sightings at Kingsley House, and the cryptic warnings at the church all pointed toward one thing: someone was using the village's ancient history to mask a very modern crime.

Driven by a restless urge to know, Simon and Debbie found themselves standing before the heavy oak door of Kingsley House. It was late evening, and the house seemed to lean into the shadows, its windows like hollow eyes.

"We shouldn't be here, Simon," Debbie whispered, her breath hitching.

"I know," he replied, his voice low. "But after what happened in the quarry, I can't just sit in the hotel and wait. That man wasn't just a victim; he was a witness. And that piece of wood… it's not just a relic. It's evidence."

They had noticed the side door—a heavy, weathered thing—was slightly ajar, the latch hanging loose. As they slipped inside, the air was thick with the scent of old paper and damp stone. The floorboards groaned under their weight, a rhythmic protest that felt like the house itself was exhaling.

In the corner of a study that looked as though it hadn't been touched since the Victorian era, Simon saw it: a small, iron-bound chest, partially hidden beneath a pile of moth-eaten tapestries. He pulled it free, and as the lid creaked open, the beam of his torch revealed not gold or jewels, but a collection of stolen artefacts—and at the bottom, a logbook.

Chapter 14: The Unmasking

The logbook belonged to the local historical society, but the entries were not about preservation. They were about extraction. Names, dates, and locations of high-value historical items being moved from local sites—the Minster, the church, even the private collections of Barnack's oldest families.

A shadow fell across the doorway.

"It's a shame, really," a calm, cultured voice said. "The history of this place is so much more valuable when it's sold piece by piece, rather than left to rot in a museum."

Simon swung the torch around. Standing in the doorway was a man they had seen only briefly at the church—not the old man, but the one who had been assisting the police with "local context." Mr. Sterling, the local archivist.

"You're the one," Simon said, his heart hammering. "The one who's been using the legends of 'haunted' houses and 'moving shadows' to keep people away from the sites you're looting."

Sterling stepped into the room, his expression devoid of the warmth he had shown earlier. He held a heavy, iron fire poker, the weight of it evident in the way he gripped it. "The 'shadows' are very effective, Mr. Goodwin. People see what they expect to see. They see ghosts; they don't see a man with a plan."

The confrontation was brief and terrifying. As Sterling lunged, the silence of Kingsley House was shattered by the sounds of a struggle. Debbie, acting on instinct, grabbed a heavy ceramic vase from a nearby pedestal, swinging it with all her might. It struck Sterling's arm, sending the poker clattering to the floor.

In the chaos, Simon managed to trigger the silent alarm they had spotted in the hallway. As the sirens began to wail in the distance, echoing through the hollows of the village, the weight of the mystery finally began to lift.

Chapter 15: The Echo of the Stone

The aftermath was a blur of flashing blue lights and the arrival of Sergeant Miller. Sterling was led away in handcuffs, his composure finally shattered, replaced by a bitter, hollow silence.

As the sun rose over the Hills and Holes the following morning, the village of Barnack felt different. The heaviness was still there—the limestone would always be heavy—but the oppressive sense of being watched had vanished. The "shadows" were just shadows again.

Simon and Debbie sat on a stone bench overlooking the quarry, watching the first light of day touch the pale, golden peaks of the hills.

"I think I've had enough of ancient history for a while," Debbie said, leaning her head on Simon's shoulder.

Simon smiled, taking her hand. "Me too. Next time, let's go somewhere with a little more concrete and a little less… character."

"Like a modern resort in Florida?" she teased.

"Exactly. Somewhere where the only thing old is the cocktail menu."

They watched the light move across the landscape, the stones of Barnack standing silent and eternal. The mystery was solved, the secrets were laid bare, but as the wind swept through the hollows, it felt as if the earth itself was finally settling—breathing a quiet sigh of relief into the morning air.
